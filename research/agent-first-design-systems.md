# Agents turn design systems into context infrastructure

The current consensus among design-system practitioners and tool vendors is that the design system has gained a second user, the AI coding or prototyping agent, and that this user needs the system's decisions in structured, machine-readable and checkable form rather than as prose written for people. Three things drive the shift. Agents have moved from autocomplete to building whole interfaces. The Model Context Protocol (MCP) has given them a standard way to fetch context. And enterprises already have a governance problem: in zeroheight's 2026 survey, **59% of design-system teams see UI that bypasses the system** and **only 17% rate their documentation "very AI-ready"**. In practice the work is a layered stack: semantic DTCG tokens; component manifests generated from types and JSDoc; a short, always-loaded instruction file that routes agents to on-demand detail through MCP servers, skills or command-line tools; and deterministic lint and test loops that catch what the model gets wrong. Good results look like agents importing real components with real props and tokens, using fewer tokens and turns per task, and creating fewer support requests for the design-system team. Atlassian, Indeed, Miro and Storybook all report gains on these measures. But almost every positive number is self-reported by a vendor or by the team itself, and much of it reaches us through one conference organiser. The independent evidence, mostly academic studies of generative UI and accessibility audits, largely documents failures. The recurring pitfalls are about context rather than model capability: hallucinated APIs, invented spacing when only component lookups are available, bloated Markdown, agents ignoring the tools they were given, documentation drift, homogenised output, and confident design rationales that don't match what was built. The MCP servers design-system teams build are typically thin, mostly read-only layers over artifacts they already ship. Atlassian found that its agents preferred a CLI to its own MCP server, which suggests the delivery channel matters less than having a single generated source of truth behind it.

## Most of the evidence comes from people selling the shift

Before getting into the substance, a reader should know where the evidence comes from, because it shapes how much weight each claim can bear. The vocabulary of "agentic", "AI-ready", "context-based" and "machine-readable" design systems is promoted mainly by a small group: consultancies and educators (Brad Frost and Big Medium, TJ Pitre's Southleft, Into Design Systems), documentation and tooling vendors (zeroheight, Supernova, Knapsack, Storybook and Chromatic, Figma) and component distributors (shadcn and Vercel's v0). Most of them sell courses, conference tickets, MCP products or documentation platforms tied to the shift ([Into Design Systems](https://www.intodesignsystems.com/agentic-design-systems); [Brad Frost course](https://aianddesign.systems/)). None of the sources identifies who coined "agent-first" or "agentic design system".

The named enterprise case studies (Indeed, Spotify, Miro, GitHub, New York State) come overwhelmingly from talks at the Into Design Systems conference, summarised on the organiser's own blog. That creates a **concentration risk**: these are curated, self-reported results that no one has checked independently ([Into Design Systems blog](https://www.intodesignsystems.com/blog/design-system-not-ready-for-ai-agents)). Atlassian's engineering posts are the most transparent first-party evidence, because they describe their methods, but they are still a team grading its own work. The truly independent evidence is thin: a handful of arXiv and ACM papers, plus accessibility audits that are themselves often run by accessibility vendors.

This report labels claims accordingly. **Vendor** means a company writing about its own product. **Practitioner** means a team or conference speaker reporting their own results. **Independent** means academic work or a third party with no product at stake. **Secondhand** means the figure reached the research through a summary, aggregator or snippet rather than the original source.

## The design system's newest user reads JSON, not prose

### A new user, and a new vocabulary

The cleanest statement of the idea comes from Diana Wolosin of Indeed: "AI is a new user. And as a new user, our design system needs to be in a format that the AI can understand", adding that "JSON is like a contract" (practitioner, via [Into Design Systems](https://www.intodesignsystems.com/agentic-design-systems)). Into Design Systems' founder Sil Bormüller defines an agentic design system as "infrastructure that gives AI agents machine-readable context about a company's components, design tokens, and rules", so that agents can "observe, flag, propose, and fix" in a loop people oversee. Older systems, by this account, were written for humans. An agentic one "encodes intent, relationships, and constraints" ([Into Design Systems](https://www.intodesignsystems.com/agentic-design-systems)). Brad Frost made the same argument in December 2025, writing that "AI is rapidly reshaping who (or what) uses your design system" and that systems which don't become machine-readable infrastructure risk becoming outdated ([bradfrost.com](https://bradfrost.com/blog/link/agentic-design-systems-in-2026-with-brad-frost/)). Big Medium frames well-structured systems as both "people-ready and AI-ready" and tells teams to tidy their Figma files first ([Big Medium](https://bigmedium.com/ideas/links/cascade-effect-in-context-based-design-systems.html)). shadcn/ui now lists "AI-Ready" as a core principle alongside open code and composition ([ssojet](https://ssojet.com/blog/what-is-shadcn-ui)).

The terms are used almost interchangeably. Where "agentic" means something extra, it usually adds the idea that agents *maintain* the system as well as consume it. Bormüller's self-healing loop (observe, detect, suggest, fix, learn) borrows from IBM's 2003 MAPE-K autonomic computing framework ([Into Design Systems](https://www.intodesignsystems.com/agentic-design-systems)). In a fragmentary LinkedIn snippet, Nathan Curtis is credited with calling a setup in which tooling catches what the LLM won't a "design harness" rather than a design system. That attribution is unverified, but the phrase captures where the field is heading ([LinkedIn snippet](https://gsmarenas.netlify.app/host-https-www.linkedin.com/posts/drboolean_no-one-in-the-design-community-seems-to-be-activity-7444046410993356800-uPjU)).

### Twelve months of infrastructure

The trigger was agents moving from suggesting lines of code to building UI on their own, combined with a standard way to hand them context. The infrastructure arrived fast. Figma's Dev Mode MCP server was reported to enter public beta around **June 2025**, returning tokens, variables, styles and Code Connect mappings so generated code matches the design system ([AlternativeTo](https://alternativeto.net/news/2025/6/figma-launches-dev-mode-mcp-server-for-direct-ai-access-to-design-data); [Figma help](https://help.figma.com/hc/en-us/articles/35281350665623)). The W3C Design Tokens Community Group published its first stable specification on **28 October 2025** ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/community/design-tokens/2025/10/28/design-tokens-specification-reaches-first-stable-version/)). Storybook previewed its MCP server in **November 2025** and shipped it for React in Storybook 10.3 around **March 2026** ([Storybook](https://storybook.js.org/blog/storybook-mcp-sneak-peek/); [Storybook 10.3](https://storybook.js.org/blog/storybook-10-3/)). Chromatic then began hosting published Storybook MCP servers so several product teams could query one shared system ([Chromatic](https://bayer.chromatic.com/blog/introducing-published-storybook-mcp-servers/)). According to Josef Richter, who cites Figma's announcements, Figma added a two-way integration with Claude Code in February 2026 and canvas writes via `use_figma` in March 2026 ([Josef Richter](https://josefrichter.design/blog/agentic-design-system)). By a July 2026 census of 20 systems, **17 shipped an official MCP server** (secondhand, [designsystems.one](https://www.designsystems.one/ai-ready/agent-files)).

Developer behaviour sets the backdrop. In the 2025 Stack Overflow survey (more than 49,000 respondents), about **84% used or planned to use AI tools**, but only about **31% used agents**. Trust in AI accuracy fell to roughly **29–33%** (outlets report slightly different figures), and only **38% of agent users said agents improved code quality** ([Stack Overflow](https://stackoverflow.blog/2025/07/29/developers-remain-willing-but-reluctant-to-use-ai-the-2025-developer-survey-results-are-here); [InfoWorld](https://infoworld.com/article/4031673/ai-use-among-software-developers-grows-but-trust-remains-an-issue-stack-overflow-survey.html)). Growth figures quoted at Into Design Systems by Romina Kavcic, such as MCP downloads rising from 100,000 to more than 8 million in under a year, are speaker claims, not checked figures ([Into Design Systems](https://www.intodesignsystems.com/agentic-design-systems)).

### Why the design system is the answer agents need

The core argument is that agents without design-system context drift toward the statistical average of their training data. Storybook puts it bluntly: without context, agents produce "unmergable slop: wrong props, bogus states, and render errors". They guess at patterns, and pointing them at `/components` or `node_modules` floods the context window with tokens ([Storybook](https://storybook.js.org/blog/storybook-mcp-sneak-peek/)). Training data cannot contain an organisation's own decisions. IBM explains its Carbon MCP server on the grounds that answers from training data miss current guidance ([Carbon](https://carbondesignsystem.com/developing/carbon-mcp/overview/)). Even within its home ecosystem, v0 "is specifically trained on the default implementations of the shadcn/ui components and may struggle with any customizations" (vendor, [v0 docs](https://v0.app/docs/design-systems-legacy)). An enterprise system that has customised a popular base is exactly the case where the model's priors work against you. Kavcic's summary line is "AI generates code, design systems generate understanding". Jan Six of GitHub adds that "the invisible part of your system is way bigger than your visible part" ([Into Design Systems](https://www.intodesignsystems.com/agentic-design-systems)).

### The enterprise pain is already measurable

The second half of the argument is organisational rather than technical. zeroheight's *State of AI in Design Systems 2026* surveyed 123 practitioners, mostly in-house design leaders at mid-to-large tech companies. It does not disclose its method or fieldwork dates. It found **82% using AI** and **48% already running an MCP server**, yet only **17% rating their documentation "very AI-ready"**. **59% reported UI that bypasses the design system**, **50% said other teams' AI use was causing real problems**, and **44% said their organisations tolerate shadow AI**. Design generation scored a **net satisfaction of −53**, the worst of any use. The report's advice is "Documentation first. MCP second. Quality over adoption." ([zeroheight](https://zeroheight.com/resources/state-of-ai-in-design-systems/)). zeroheight's separate *Design Systems Report 2026* puts AI use at **56%**, with **only 15% saying AI lives up to the hype**. That comes from webinar copy, not the full report, and the gap between 56% and 82% probably reflects different samples or questions ([zeroheight webinar](https://webinars.zeroheight.com/register/zeroheight-design-systems-report-2026-ai-and-design-systems-the-promise-and-the-reality)). Whichever figure is right, both reports show design-system teams adopting AI faster than they are getting value from it, and naming documentation and machine-readability as the bottleneck. On this reading, "agent-first" is less a productivity promise than a governance response: AI is already generating off-system UI across the organisation, and the design system is the only lever the design-system team controls.

### The skeptics have a point, and it reinforces the case

The skeptical camp is real. Christopher Butler calls AI "very likely overhyped to the point of catastrophe" and argues that dropping generative AI into existing design systems rarely benefits anyone ([minifeed](https://minifeed.net/blogs/dR1ul1)). An arXiv paper by Shin and colleagues argues that frictionless LLM generation speeds up convergence on dominant aesthetics and proposes "productive friction" as a remedy. It is conceptual and not peer-reviewed (independent, [arXiv 2603.13036](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13036)). A summary of zeroheight's report says Gartner's 2025 Hype Cycle places design systems sliding toward the trough of disillusionment. That is secondhand ([Briefly](https://briefly-prod.herokuapp.com/anchor/UX_design/story/design-systems-report-2026---brought-to-you-by-zeroheight)). The most useful counterpoint comes from Frances Liddell, whose post title says it all: "A design system that isn't reliable for people won't be reliable for AI" ([Frances Liddell](https://www.francesliddell.com/my-blog/a-design-system-that-isn-t-reliable-for-people-won-t-be-reliable-for-ai)). The two camps agree more than they appear to. Skeptics doubt the quality of generated output, and the agent-first answer is that quality depends on the context the system supplies. A thin system built on library defaults may make homogenisation worse. A rich one that encodes a distinct brand is the main proposed antidote.

## Agent-first systems are built in layers, from tokens up to validation loops

Across the sources, a consistent architecture has emerged. No single vendor prescribes it, but the leading systems converge on it:

| Layer | What it holds | Common formats | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tokens | Values plus their purpose | DTCG 2025.10 JSON, CSS variables | Atlassian tokens, SLDS styling hooks |
| Component metadata | API, variants, when to use, alternatives | Storybook manifest, Code Connect, custom-elements manifest, JSDoc | Storybook `components.json`, Carbon Code Connect |
| Always-on instructions | Foundations and routing to detail | AGENTS.md, CLAUDE.md, Copilot instructions, Cursor rules | Carbon AGENTS.md, Primer Copilot instructions |
| On-demand detail | Component docs, patterns, icons | MCP server, skills, CLI, split llms.txt | Atlassian ADS MCP, CLI and skills |
| Portable snapshot | A single-file summary for tools you don't control | DESIGN.md, llms-full.txt | atlassian.design/DESIGN.md |
| Verification | Deterministic checks the agent runs | ESLint, Stylelint, type checks, component and a11y tests | `@atlaskit/eslint-plugin-design-system`, Storybook `test-run` |

The principle that ties these together is "metadata in code": types, JSDoc and token descriptions live in source and are *generated* into every downstream channel, so the documentation agents read cannot drift from the API they call.

### Semantic tokens tell a model what a value is for

The DTCG specification reached its first stable version, **2025.10**, on 28 October 2025. It has Format, Color and Resolver modules, and the Resolver module handles themes and modes ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/community/design-tokens/2025/10/28/design-tokens-specification-reaches-first-stable-version/); [Design Tokens TR](https://www.designtokens.org/tr/2025.10/)). Accounts of its backing vary: Richter says more than 24 organisations, and a secondary blog says more than 40. Both are secondhand ([Josef Richter](https://josefrichter.design/blog/agentic-design-system); [codercops](https://blog.codercops.com/blog/design-tokens-2026-w3c-format-guide)). Some agent-skill pages still call 2025.10 a draft. They are out of date ([skills.cat](https://skills.cat/skills/tyler-r-kendrick/agent-skills/design-tokens)).

For agents, the file format matters less than **semantic naming**. A model that picks `blue-600` cannot be linted for intent. A model that uses `color.text.danger` for non-danger text can be caught. The DTCG `$description` field is the cheapest place to put "when to use" guidance a model will read ([DTCG format module](https://www.designtokens.org/tr/2025.10/format/)). Enforcement already exists at scale. Atlassian's ESLint plugin flags hard-coded values (`ensure-design-token-usage`) and unsafe token usage, which it says "risks the health of the system" ([Atlassian](https://atlassian.design/components/eslint-plugin-design-system/ensure-design-token-usage)). Salesforce's SLDS 2 ESLint plugin flags private and deprecated styling hooks ([socket.dev](https://socket.dev/npm/package/@salesforce-ux/eslint-plugin-slds/overview/1.0.3-internal)). Some platforms are dropping tokens as a separate input. v0's Design Systems 2.0 learns them from source code and the package ([v0](https://v0.app/docs/design-systems-2)). A frequently repeated figure, that token adoption rose from 56% to 84% of teams, traces to a zeroheight survey cited without a link or method, so treat it as weak ([Josef Richter](https://josefrichter.design/blog/agentic-design-system)).

### Component manifests generated from code keep docs from drifting

The machine-readable component API is converging on JSON manifests generated from TypeScript types and JSDoc. Storybook's manifests are "JSON objects that describe the contents of your Storybook in a concise, structured way that is easy for AI agents to understand". There are two: `/manifests/components.json`, which holds names, descriptions, props with types and defaults, and usage snippets from stories, and `/manifests/docs.json` for MDX content. Teams can exclude instruction-only or deprecated stories with a `!manifest` tag ([Storybook manifests](https://storybook.js.org/docs/ai/manifests.md)). Sources disagree on whether the manifest is on by default. A March 2026 pull request made `componentsManifest` default to true, but Storybook's 10.6 MCP docs tell users to enable it explicitly, so check against your version ([PR #33974](https://github.com/storybookjs/storybook/pull/33974); [Storybook MCP overview](https://storybook.js.org/docs/ai/mcp/overview)). Framework support is uneven. React and Angular-on-Vite are fully supported, Vue needs an experimental docgen server, and Angular Webpack and Nuxt get no manifest ([Storybook MCP overview](https://storybook.js.org/docs/ai/mcp/overview)). Enterprises running several frameworks should check this early.

Storybook's single most transferable authoring rule is that every component and prop description should state its purpose *and point to the alternative*. Its example says Button is for interactions that don't navigate, and navigation should use Link ([Storybook AI best practices](https://storybook.js.org/docs/ai/best-practices)). Allow-lists matter as much as descriptions, because hallucinated components are the dominant failure. Ant Design's Copilot instructions include an export allow-list "so models stop inventing Box, Stack and Container" (secondhand, [designsystems.one](https://www.designsystems.one/ai-ready/agent-files)). For web-component systems, the custom-elements manifest plays the same role, describing attributes, events, slots and CSS custom properties ([custom-elements-manifest](https://github.com/webcomponents/custom-elements-manifest)). Figma Code Connect maps Figma components to production code so that Dev Mode and MCP consumers see real snippets ([Figma Code Connect](https://developers.figma.com/docs/code-connect/)). Adoption is low, though: only 2 of 20 surveyed systems shipped it in July 2026, IBM Carbon among them (secondhand, [designsystems.one](https://www.designsystems.one/ai-ready/agent-files)). Atlassian has taken the principle furthest, keeping component APIs, usage guidance, tokens, icons, accessibility guidance and lint rules "in typed schemas alongside the code" and generating one dataset from them (practitioner, [Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/ai-at-work/giving-ai-agents-design-system-context-from-the-terminal-what-we-learned-building-a-cli)).

### A short router file points agents to on-demand detail

Teams now ship layered agent documentation. A short, always-loaded instruction file routes the agent to detailed material it fetches only when needed. Into Design Systems calls this progressive disclosure. Always-on rules cover foundations such as spacing, colour and type. MCP fetches component detail on demand. AGENTS.md coordinates the two and sets governance ([Into Design Systems](https://www.intodesignsystems.com/agentic-design-systems)). Figma's guidance for Make takes the same approach: a `guidelines/` folder with a `Guidelines.md` that is read first and *routes* rather than holds everything, plus many short files for tokens, components and composition, so Make loads only what it needs ([Figma help](https://help.figma.com/hc/articles/43602393097239)). Atlassian's hand-curated `llms.txt` links six topic files rather than one dump ([atlassian.design/llms.txt](https://atlassian.design/llms.txt)).

An August 2026 census of public repositories shows how unevenly this has landed (secondhand, [designsystems.one](https://www.designsystems.one/ai-ready/agent-files)):

| System | AGENTS.md | CLAUDE.md | Cursor rules | Copilot instructions | Skills | MCP |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| GitHub Primer | No | No | No | Yes | Yes (14) | Yes |
| IBM Carbon | Yes | No | No | No | Yes | Yes |
| shadcn/ui | Stub | No | No | No | Yes | Yes |
| Ant Design | Yes | No | No | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Adobe React Spectrum | Yes | Stub | No | No | Yes | No |
| MUI | Yes | Stub | No | No | No | No |
| Ark UI | No | Yes | Yes | No | No | No |
| Radix Primitives | Yes | No | No | No | No | No |
| Chakra UI | No | Yes | No | No | No | Yes |

The census also turned up details worth copying. Carbon's AGENTS.md says it must stay short because of token cost. Primer's Copilot instructions cross-reference its 14 skills by filename. shadcn/ui's skill ships an `evals/` directory that regression-tests the skill's own rules. It also found a quiet wiring failure. Spectrum's and MUI's CLAUDE.md files *link* to AGENTS.md in Markdown instead of importing it with `@AGENTS.md`, so according to the author Claude Code never loads the real file. The census counted no native AGENTS.md support across 376 Claude Code releases ([designsystems.one](https://www.designsystems.one/ai-ready/agent-files)). If your agent files target several tools, make sure each tool actually loads them.

### Single-file formats travel well but underperform

DESIGN.md, a format from Google Labs built for Stitch, puts machine-readable tokens in YAML front matter and design intent in a prose body. It is Apache-2.0 licensed, and its repository calls it alpha with "format changes expected" ([Google blog](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/google-labs/stitch-design-md/)). Reports that Google open-sourced it in April 2026 are secondhand. Richter calls it "real but alpha" and passes on reviewers' view that it is "not yet a safe production dependency" ([Josef Richter](https://josefrichter.design/blog/agentic-design-system)).

Atlassian's June 2026 test on a production login screen is the clearest public comparison of delivery formats. The authors call it "not conclusive" (practitioner, [Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/how-we-build/atlassians-design-md-is-here-what-we-learned-testing-portable-design-context-in-practice)):

| Approach | Design-system context used | Average tokens | Average time | Average turns |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| No context | about 5% | 4.20M | 6m19s | 43 |
| Atlassian MCP | about 80% | 3.75M | 5m01s | 35.1 |
| Atlassian skill | about 80% | 4.43M | 5m23s | 36 |
| DESIGN.md | about 30% | 7.21M | 6m46s | 45.3 |

The DESIGN.md file was 80 KB, about 19,800 tokens, compared with roughly 2.5 MB of guidance behind the MCP server and skill. Fitting the file meant cutting usage guidance for more than 50 components. Because the file described component internals, agents *rebuilt* components instead of importing `@atlaskit/button`. Atlassian still publishes DESIGN.md for portability, customer theming and blue-sky prototyping in tools it doesn't control, and used it with Figma Make for its Team '26 keynote dashboards, where output moved "from generic to recognizably Atlassian". Its verdict is that if the agent supports MCP or skills, use those ([Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/how-we-build/atlassians-design-md-is-here-what-we-learned-testing-portable-design-context-in-practice)).

### Distribution is moving from copies of docs to pointers at verifiable source

shadcn's open-code registry, which copies component source into the consuming repo and describes it with `registry.json`, became the default distribution format for AI generation because the model can read and edit the actual code ([v0 legacy docs](https://v0.app/docs/design-systems)). Vercel has since labelled that flow legacy. v0's Design Systems 2.0 imports GitHub repos (including real consumer apps, which show providers, fonts and theme setup), npm packages, Figma frames and Storybook links. It saves the result as a skill: a `SKILL.md`, a `v0.json` pointing to up to three read-only source repositories, and an approved starter app. The skill is "an adapter that points v0 to your source", not a copy of the docs, and if a component, prop or token can't be verified from source, v0 should not use it. A human reviews the generated starter before approval, and v0 re-checks it after every edit (vendor, [v0 Design Systems 2.0](https://v0.app/docs/design-systems-2)). Figma's Make kits, announced in April 2026, let system authors package a production component package with guidelines so Make output "starts from production-aligned components". Figma admits it is "still working toward" replicating Figma-library component structures in Make, so code packages are the production path (vendor, [Figma blog](https://www.figma.com/blog/introducing-make-kits-and-make-attachments/); [Figma help](https://help.figma.com/hc/en-us/articles/35946832653975-Use-your-design-system-package-in-Make-kits)).

One finding should temper expectations for bespoke systems. In a June 2026 comparison relayed by the census, Meta's Astryx scored 87 overall against 94 for a plain shadcn/ui baseline, which won 8 of 10 prompts. This is secondhand and was not traced to the original ([designsystems.one](https://www.designsystems.one/ai-ready/agent-files)). If it holds, models' prior training on shadcn and Tailwind conventions is a real asset. A heavily custom enterprise system pays for its uniqueness in context, which is a strong argument for investing in that context rather than hoping the model will cope.

### Rules work best when they are imperative, testable and lint-backed

Teams encode intent as short, checkable rules rather than principles. Figma's guidance is the most detailed public playbook. Use imperative, specific language ("Do not use small text for anything except captions" rather than "use small text sparingly"). Require the agent to read a component's guideline before using it. List invalid names and forbidden patterns. Provide decision trees from intent to component and variant. Show correct and wrong code side by side. State hierarchy rules such as one primary button per section ([Figma help](https://help.figma.com/hc/articles/43602393097239)). Kaelig Deloumeau-Prigent's July 2026 dataset of "coercion techniques" across systems counts **31 validation-loop, 28 prohibition, 23 curated-context, 22 tool-gating and 15 token-enforcement** entries, so prohibition and validation dominate ([State of AI in Design Systems](https://state-of-ai-in-design-systems.netlify.app/systems/atlassian-design-system)). The test of a good model-facing rule is whether a machine could check it. "No raw hex values" and "one primary per section" pass. "Be calm and trustworthy" does not.

### Deterministic checks are the cheapest guardrail

The highest-leverage guardrail costs nothing in context. Atlassian notes that its ESLint rules "enforce standards for humans and agents at no token cost, creating a feedback loop that improves agent output" ([Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/how-we-build/atlassians-design-md-is-here-what-we-learned-testing-portable-design-context-in-practice)). Supernova recommends making tokens mandatory and failing CI on hard-coded values (vendor, [Supernova](https://www.supernova.io/blog/from-prototype-to-product-how-design-systems-prevent-the-vibe-coding-pitfalls)). The loop the sources point to is this. The agent generates code. It then runs type checks, lint rules for tokens, deprecations and allow-listed imports, Storybook interaction and accessibility tests, and ideally visual regression. It reads the errors, fixes them and repeats. Lint messages should name the correct token or component so the fix is mechanical. The MCP specification now supports this pattern directly: since the 2025-11-25 revision, input validation failures should come back as tool errors "to enable model self-correction" ([MCP changelog](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-11-25/changelog)). Two relayed findings show why automated accessibility checks are not enough on their own. A CHI 2026 study reportedly found about 1.8 semantic accessibility issues per generated UI, all of which passed axe-core. A 2024 study found deprecated-API usage of 25–38% across seven models. Both are secondhand via the census ([designsystems.one](https://www.designsystems.one/ai-ready/agent-files)).

## MCP servers for design systems are thin layers over artifacts teams already ship

### What MCP gives a design-system team

MCP is an open JSON-RPC protocol that Anthropic launched in November 2024 ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/model-context-protocol)). A server exposes three kinds of primitive. **Tools** are functions the model calls, such as "get component" or "validate code". **Resources** are data addressed by URI, such as a token file. **Prompts** are templates the user invokes ([MCP spec](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-11-25)). Servers run locally over stdio, typically launched with `npx`, or remotely over Streamable HTTP ([MCP transports](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-11-25/basic/transports)). The 2025-11-25 revision added several things a design-system server can use. Icons can be attached to tools. There is guidance on tool names. URL-mode elicitation and OAuth Client ID Metadata Documents help hosted, authenticated servers. Experimental tasks handle long-running work. And validation errors now come back as tool errors the model can learn from ([MCP changelog](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-11-25/changelog)). MCP Apps (SEP-1865), proposed in November 2025, lets a server return interactive UI through `ui://` resources rendered in sandboxed iframes. For design systems, that opens the door to live component previews or token swatches inside a chat client ([MCP blog](https://blog.modelcontextprotocol.io/posts/2025-11-21-mcp-apps/)).

### The landscape splits into canvas, code, distribution and system-of-record servers

### How teams build their own

The best public walkthrough comes from James Ives, lead software engineer on a design-systems team at Blizzard Entertainment (the system is not named). It is a Node server built on the official TypeScript SDK (`McpServer`, `StdioServerTransport`, zod-described parameters). It reads three sources the team already maintained: the custom-elements manifest, the `*.stories.ts` files parsed with the TypeScript compiler, and written docs. It exposes three tools: `list-components`, `get-component` and `get-component-examples` ([dev.to](https://dev.to/jamesives/your-design-system-needs-an-mcp-server-4c7a)). Python's FastMCP is the alternative SDK route ([gofastmcp.com](https://gofastmcp.com)).

That shape generalises. First-party servers cluster around four capabilities: list or search; get a component's docs and props; get tokens and icons; and, less often, validate or migrate. They wrap artifacts the release process already produces: component manifests, DTCG token JSON, Markdown or MDX docs, and live APIs like Figma's. Code-oriented servers tend to run locally over stdio via `npx` (Primer, shadcn, Chakra, Storybook in development). Documentation platforms and canvases are hosted over HTTP (Figma remote, Carbon, Supernova, published Storybook). Live canvas writes go through bridge plugins (Southleft). Retrieval is keyword or name lookup. No design-system server documented embedding-based search. Versioning is handled inconsistently. A community Mantine server pins `MANTINE_VERSION`, zeroheight exposes `list-releases`, and Primer installs `@latest` ([Mantine](https://github.com/hakxel/mantine-ui-server); [Supernova](https://www.supernova.io/blog/design-system-mcp-servers); [Primer](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/@primer/mcp@0.3.4/README.md)). The most robust pattern, inferred from the manifest-based designs rather than stated by any one team, is to generate the server's data in the design system's own release pipeline and publish it with the package version, so the server cannot drift from the shipped library. Atlassian does a version of this. Its CLI, MCP server, skill generator and DESIGN.md generator share the same handlers, so there is "no possible drift" between channels ([Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/ai-at-work/giving-ai-agents-design-system-context-from-the-terminal-what-we-learned-building-a-cli)).

### Tool design follows a few well-tested rules

Anthropic's engineering guidance on writing tools for agents is the most cited general reference. Use fewer, non-overlapping tools, and consolidate multi-step flows into one call. Namespace them clearly. Paginate, filter and truncate responses (Claude Code caps a tool response at 25,000 tokens by default). Offer a concise or detailed `response_format`; in the post's example, concise used about a third of the tokens. Return human-readable names rather than IDs. Write actionable errors. Describe tools as if onboarding a new hire ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/writing-tools-for-agents)). Design-system servers apply these in specific ways. Storybook's `docs-show` returns a component's props, its first three stories and an index of the rest, and agents see only a story's summary or the first 60 characters of its description, because too much irrelevant context "can overwhelm them and degrade performance" ([Storybook MCP](https://storybook.js.org/docs/ai/mcp/overview); [best practices](https://storybook.js.org/docs/ai/best-practices)). Ives recommends an index tool first and detail tools second, a description for every parameter, and an explicit "not found" rather than an empty result ([dev.to](https://dev.to/jamesives/your-design-system-needs-an-mcp-server-4c7a)). Shopify enforces a docs-first step: `learn_shopify_api` returns a `conversationId` that other tools require ([Docker Hub, Shopify MCP](https://hub.docker.com/r/mcp/shopify)). Figma ships a `create_design_system_rules` prompt that writes a rules file into the repo, and Storybook ships a sample AGENTS.md telling agents to check props through its docs tools and run `test-run` ([Figma tools](https://developers.figma.com/docs/figma-mcp-server/tools-and-prompts/); [Storybook MCP](https://storybook.js.org/docs/ai/mcp/overview)). Tool counts range from 4 (zeroheight) through 9 (Storybook) and about 32 (Figma) to 130 (Southleft). For a single design system, the first-party examples suggest **three to eight tools**: list, search, get component, get tokens, get examples and validate.

### Agents don't use a server just because it exists

The most important practical lesson from first-party teams is that building an MCP server does not guarantee agents will call it. Miro's six-person design-system team saw Slack support questions fall by about **70–80%** only after it put explicit instructions in the root Claude file telling the agent to call the design-system MCP ("people were getting answers in their IDE"). This is a practitioner report from a talk, and the full write-up is paywalled ([Into Design Systems Substack](https://intodesignsystems.substack.com/p/miro-ai-design-system)). Atlassian's transcripts showed agents sometimes ignoring instructions and reading source files directly, behaviour the metrics dashboard did not reveal. When it offered a CLI alongside the MCP server, **CLI usage passed MCP usage by 60%**. Against a skill-plus-MCP baseline, the skill-plus-CLI setup was about **8% faster (352s to 325s) and used about 8% fewer tokens (218k to 201k)**. Pre-installing the CLI instead of starting it through `npx` cut more than 15 seconds of overhead per query by about 95%. Atlassian keeps MCP as the fallback for sandboxes without terminal access. It also printed the next likely command in each result, so agents stopped re-reading the skill or running `--help` (practitioner, [Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/ai-at-work/giving-ai-agents-design-system-context-from-the-terminal-what-we-learned-building-a-cli)). The broader MCP-versus-CLI argument is live. One top Hacker News comment says MCP has no real-world benefit, and a maintainer replies "use whatever gets the job done". That is a snippet from a thread the research did not open ([HN mirror](https://astro-hackernews.netlify.app/stories/47400261)). The defensible position is that MCP is one delivery channel among several. What matters is a single generated source of truth, exposed through whichever channels your agents actually use, with root instruction files that make calling it mandatory.

### Testing a server means testing agent behaviour, not endpoints

MCP Inspector is the standard interactive debugger for checking that tools respond correctly ([MCP Inspector](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/docs/tools/inspector)). Correct responses are not the same as useful ones. Anthropic recommends realistic multi-step tasks with verifiable outcomes, run in programmatic agent loops, tracking tool calls, tokens and errors, with an agent analysing the transcripts ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/writing-tools-for-agents)). Atlassian's benchmark follows this pattern. It uses real front-end tasks graded automatically, the same agent and model each run, and one variable at a time, and the team found the full transcripts "more useful than the dashboard" ([Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/ai-at-work/giving-ai-agents-design-system-context-from-the-terminal-what-we-learned-building-a-cli)). Spotify's Encore team reportedly built an evaluation framework that runs prompts across several LLMs and compares the output in code and visually: "We can't just launch and hope for the best" (practitioner, [Into Design Systems blog](https://www.intodesignsystems.com/blog/design-system-not-ready-for-ai-agents)). No design-system team has published its evaluation harness.

### Security favours read-only defaults and pinned versions

MCP has a well-documented attack surface. Invariant Labs showed tool poisoning (instructions hidden in tool descriptions that the model sees and the user doesn't, used in a proof of concept to steal SSH keys through Cursor), rug pulls (a tool changed after approval), cross-server shadowing, and a hijack of the GitHub MCP server through prompt injection in public issues ([Invariant Labs](https://invariantlabs.ai/blog/mcp-security-notification)). According to Cloud Security Alliance summaries, the OWASP MCP Top 10 lists tool poisoning as MCP03:2025, and CVE-2025-54136 (CVSS 8.8) showed that approving a tool once does not protect against later server-side changes ([CSA](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-mcp-tool-poisoning-ai-agent-exfiltration-2/)). No incident involving a design-system server has been reported. But design-system servers often serve user-editable content (docs pages, Figma layer names, story descriptions), and that is the same indirect-injection surface as the GitHub issues attack. The sensible enterprise posture follows the examples already in the market. Published Storybooks on Chromatic expose only read-only docs tools, Southleft offers a read-only remote mode, Anthropic recommends annotating destructive tools, and teams should pin versions rather than install `@latest` ([Chromatic](https://box.chromatic.com/docs/mcp); [Southleft](https://github.com/southleft/figma-console-mcp); [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/writing-tools-for-agents)). Keeping read and write servers separate is the next step for anything that can edit the canvas or the repository.

## Good results are real components, fewer tokens and fewer tickets, mostly by self-report

### What "good" means in practice

Definitions of success are largely qualitative. Figma describes the goal as the system becoming a "carrier of craft". It should encode taste so that AI applies it at creation, enable "grounded exploration" that stays on-system, and make implicit knowledge explicit, because "AI doesn't infer context the way people do" (vendor, [Figma](https://www.figma.com/blog/5-shifts-redefining-design-systems-in-the-ai-era/)). In practice, teams measure good through proxies: correct component and API use, token adherence, tokens and time per task, fewer support requests, and prototype volume. No one has published a direct measure of whether AI output is on-brand or maintainable.

Read together, these results say something more specific than "MCP helps". The gains vary by an order of magnitude depending on what changed. Adding structured context where there was none produced the largest numbers (Storybook's 2.76x). Changing the *format* of context, from Markdown to JSON, produced Indeed's 80% token cut. Changing the delivery channel, from MCP to CLI, produced Atlassian's 8%. The format and the curation of context matter at least as much as having a server at all. Prototype volume is a throughput metric that says nothing about production survival. No team has published a code-review acceptance rate or a component-coverage figure for AI-generated UI, which are the two measures an enterprise most needs.

### How the more rigorous teams measure

A workable measurement stack can be assembled from what these teams describe. First, a benchmark suite of real tasks graded automatically on pass rate, tokens, time and tool calls, changing one variable at a time (Atlassian, Indeed, Spotify). Second, static checks on output: TypeScript errors and import accuracy, as tracked in Storybook's own evals ([storybookjs #35526](https://github.com/storybookjs/storybook/issues/35526)). Third, a token-adherence ratio, meaning tokenised style values divided by all style values. Design Agent proposes regenerating below 0.95, though that threshold is the author's own, not a standard ([design-agent.dev](https://design-agent.dev/blog/design-tokens-agent-readable-language/)). Fourth, visual comparison against a reference, plus automated and manual accessibility audits. Fifth, transcript review, which caught Atlassian's tool-avoidance problem. Sixth, downstream signals such as support deflection and system adoption. Brad Frost's course models a readiness audit that scores a system before any of this. His demo system scored 70/100 before fixes (practitioner, secondhand, [Brad Frost](https://bradfrost.com/newsletter/archive/ai-and-design-systems-chapter-3-is-done/)).

### The independent evidence points the other way

The asymmetry is stark. Nearly every positive number in this field is self-reported, while the independent studies mostly document failures of generative UI in general. The "Design Theater" benchmark tested five generative UI tools on 24 tasks producing 120 interfaces. **Over 25% of stated design rationales were not implemented**, rising to **34% for functional requirements**. The tools recognised only about half the UX principles in their prompts, and four of the five implemented **6% or fewer of the functional principles**. The authors warn that a polished rationale can persuade stakeholders that a generic UI was tailored (independent, accepted at AAAI/AIES, [arXiv 2607.22928](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22928)). In an AudioEye test reported by VentureBeat, five AI tools built 15 sites and **all 15 failed WCAG Level A**, averaging 55 issues per page. AudioEye is an accessibility vendor ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/technology/we-asked-five-ai-tools-to-build-accessible-websites-all-15-sites-failed)). The academic picture is mixed. A W4A 2026 paper found polished-looking interfaces with potential WCAG violations ([ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3800424.3800430)). Another ACM paper found relatively few violations but also found that explicit WCAG prompting did not reliably help ([ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3715336.3735691)).

The agent-first thesis is that design-system grounding fixes much of this, because the defaults become well-built, accessible components rather than the model's statistical mean. That is plausible, and the case studies point that way. But **no independent, peer-reviewed study has yet compared AI UI output with and without design-system grounding**. Until one does, an enterprise should treat the productivity numbers as directional and invest in its own benchmark.

## Most failures trace back to missing, bloated or ignored context

The failure modes in the sources fall into a recognisable set, and most of them are context problems rather than model problems.

| Pitfall | What it looks like | Mitigation the sources support |
|---|---|---|
| Hallucinated components and props | Agents "poke around node_modules, filling in the gaps with best guess" | Structured get-component tool, export allow-lists, lint on imports |
| Invented foundations | A card request returns card and button metadata but no spacing, type or colour, "so the LLM invents them" | Always-on foundation rules alongside on-demand lookups |
| Hard-coded values despite MCP | An agent keeps emitting hex values with Figma MCP connected | Token lint that fails CI and names the correct token |
| Context overload | About 30,000 tokens per query, 82% coverage, still hallucinating | Structured JSON, small responses, curated examples |
| Over-describing internals | Agents rebuild components instead of importing them | Describe which component to use, not how it is built |
| Agents ignoring tools | Agents read source files directly despite instructions | Explicit root-file instructions, transcript review, a CLI channel |
| Broken wiring | CLAUDE.md links to AGENTS.md instead of importing it, so the file never loads | Verify each tool's loading behaviour |
| Drift and stale docs | Prose agent files give stale instructions | Generate every channel from one typed source |
| Too complex to reason about | Developers go to Cursor and ship off-system output | Split components into smaller, layered "context bubbles" |
| Figma-to-code mismatch | Make cannot reproduce library structures. One Code Connect framework on Professional plans | Treat the code package as the source of truth |
| Homogenised output | Inter, purple gradients, three rounded cards | A rich system that encodes a distinct brand |
| Rationale does not match the build | More than a quarter of stated rationales not implemented | Review the artifact, not the agent's explanation |
| Accessibility regressions | Semantic issues that pass automated checks | Accessible components as defaults plus manual audit |

Sources for the table: [James Ives](https://jamesiv.es/blog/your-design-system-needs-an-mcp-server/); [Into Design Systems blog](https://www.intodesignsystems.com/blog/design-system-not-ready-for-ai-agents); [dev.to](https://dev.to/karthikeyan_sridhar_ecc11/my-ai-agent-guesses-design-tokens-repeatedly-mcp-doesnt-fix-it-either-570m); [Atlassian DESIGN.md](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/how-we-build/atlassians-design-md-is-here-what-we-learned-testing-portable-design-context-in-practice); [Atlassian CLI](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/ai-at-work/giving-ai-agents-design-system-context-from-the-terminal-what-we-learned-building-a-cli); [designsystems.one](https://www.designsystems.one/ai-ready/agent-files); [Design Systems Collective](https://www.designsystemscollective.com/what-does-ai-readiness-mean-in-design-systems-5d677543bbce); [Figma help](https://help.figma.com/hc/en-us/articles/35946832653975-Use-your-design-system-package-in-Make-kits); [Figma tools](https://developers.figma.com/docs/figma-mcp-server/tools-and-prompts/); [smoothui](https://smoothui.dev/blog/ai-design-slop); [arXiv 2607.22928](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22928).

Three of these deserve more attention than a table row. The **invented-foundations** problem is the most common architectural mistake. Brad Frost's example shows that an MCP server answering only "what is a card?" leaves everything between components to the model's imagination. That is why the progressive-disclosure pattern insists on foundations in every prompt ([Into Design Systems blog](https://www.intodesignsystems.com/blog/design-system-not-ready-for-ai-agents)). The **system-complexity** problem matters most for enterprises with large, mature libraries. At Spotify, developers bypassed Encore because it was "too complex for AI to reason about". The fix was structural, splitting components into foundation, style and behaviour layers, not better documentation (practitioner, [Into Design Systems blog](https://www.intodesignsystems.com/blog/design-system-not-ready-for-ai-agents)). The **rationale gap** from Design Theater is a review risk specific to design leaders. An agent that explains its choices fluently invites reviewers to check the explanation rather than the build, and the evidence says the explanation is wrong more than a quarter of the time ([arXiv 2607.22928](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22928)).

Homogenisation needs an honest framing too. Commentators describe a "vibe coding crisis" in which design becomes a commodity because AI output is "an average of everything" ([Web Designer Depot](https://webdesignerdepot.com/the-vibe-coding-crisis-is-web-design-becoming-a-commodity/)). Built In calls AI-polished mediocrity a "new skeuomorphism" ([Built In](https://builtin.com/articles/ai-design-slop-era)). Figma itself warns that "speed without direction leads to divergence" ([Figma](https://www.figma.com/blog/5-shifts-redefining-design-systems-in-the-ai-era/)). Design Theater found convergence in layout and visual appearance across tools, with more variation in colour ([arXiv 2607.22928](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22928)). An enterprise system is a defence against this only if it carries distinctive decisions. A system that is mostly a lightly themed open-source base will converge with everyone else's.

Some commonly assumed pitfalls are not yet evidenced. "Too many MCP tools" is plausible but was not directly documented for design-system servers. The closest evidence is Atlassian's round-trip overhead and Indeed's token costs. Vendor lock-in was not discussed with evidence in any source, although Atlassian's one-source, many-channels design is a structural hedge against it.

## Governance moves from approving screens to setting agent permissions

### Trust is granted per action, not per agent

The governance model emerging from practitioners is **tiered autonomy**. Romina Kavcic proposes auto-merge for linting fixes, typos and accessibility labels; draft pull requests for changes to token values and descriptions; and suggestion-only for new APIs, breaking changes and governance itself. Agents "earn autonomy" in stages. At GitHub, Primer's agents may only create issues, never merge code, and daily QA workflows use "safe outputs" that need human review (practitioner, [Into Design Systems blog](https://www.intodesignsystems.com/blog/design-system-not-ready-for-ai-agents)). Kavcic's self-healing drift loop, which scores drift and opens pull requests automatically, is framed against her estimate that design-system teams spend 30–40% of their time on maintenance (practitioner, same source; her affiliation in that article was not verified). The broader shift is from approving components before they are built to detecting and correcting problems afterwards (agency, secondhand, [f1studioz](https://f1studioz.com/blog/blog-enterprise-design-system-governance-ai/)). Vendors including Supernova market drift-detection agents for this purpose ([Supernova](https://www.supernova.io/blog/the-future-of-enterprise-design-systems-2026-trends-and-tools-for-success)).

Rules are also moving *into* the tools, through AGENTS.md files, skills and Make kits, rather than sitting in published guidance people are expected to read. Figma notes that this widens the contributor base to people "without traditional product titles" (vendor, [Figma](https://www.figma.com/blog/5-shifts-redefining-design-systems-in-the-ai-era/)). Figma's Git integration lets non-engineers import a repo, edit code visually on the canvas and hand changes to coding agents "with built-in governance" ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/technology/are-designers-the-new-swes-figma-makes-new-two-way-github-integration-turns-designs-into-live-production-code-with-built-in-governance)). With generative UI (covered below), governance moves further still, from design time to runtime. A team can no longer review every screen, only the parts and the rules for combining them.

### The designer's job shifts toward encoding intent

The role change practitioners describe is from producing components to curating context and policy. Designers encode intent in component descriptions that explain what a component does and how it combines with others. They decide which agent actions are auto-merged and which need review. They pair with engineers rather than meeting them weekly ([Into Design Systems](https://www.intodesignsystems.com/agentic-design-systems)). Kavcic compares the design system to a CI/CD pipeline or an API through which AI builds products safely. A podcast description of Nathan Curtis's recent work says he holds that systems thinking, governance and coordination stay essential as non-deterministic workflows grow. That is a description of his views, not his own writing ([On Theme](https://www.buzzsprout.com/2417527/episodes/17864420)). The day-to-day workflow is hybridising. A common pattern is to draft in Figma Make or v0, then hand off to Claude Code or Cursor for responsiveness, accessibility and state handling ([925 Studios](https://www.925studios.co/blog/claude-code-vs-figma-make-designers-comparison-2026)). Figma and Anthropic's "Code to Canvas" (February 2026) brings agent-written code back onto the canvas ([Gigazine](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20260218-figma-code-to-canvas)). A third-party page reports that Figma's 2026 AI research found developers taking part in design rising from 44% to 60% and designers working in code nearly doubling. That has not been verified against Figma's report ([buildmvpfast](https://www.buildmvpfast.com/blog/figma-make-claude-code-solo-founder-workflow-2026)). Earlier Figma data, also via secondary coverage, found 78% of designers saying AI made them more efficient but only 47% saying it made them better at their role ([TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/pro/figmas-latest-survey-shows-ai-is-not-yet-living-up-to-its-over-hyped-promise)).

The constraint is time, not money. In zeroheight's survey, 25% of teams named "no time" as the top barrier: "Budget isn't the blocker. Time is." ([zeroheight](https://zeroheight.com/resources/state-of-ai-in-design-systems/)). No source offered data on headcount, titles or skills changing because of agent-first work. For a senior designer, the practical implication is that the unglamorous basics of naming, token semantics and component descriptions have become the highest-leverage design-system work. Those artifacts are now read thousands of times a day by agents that cannot ask follow-up questions.

## Adjacent ideas that change what a design system is for

### Generative UI turns the component library into a model's vocabulary

Coding agents are the first consumer of agent-first design systems, but generative UI may be the larger long-term one. Between April 2025 and early 2026, generative UI went from vendor experiments to a stack of overlapping protocols, and almost all of them work the same way: **the model picks components from a pre-approved catalog and fills them with data instead of writing free-form code**. CopilotKit groups the approaches into three. Controlled approaches let the agent pick from prebuilt components. Declarative approaches let the agent describe what to render in JSON. Open-ended approaches, such as sandboxed HTML apps, offer the most freedom and the least control (vendor framing, [CopilotKit](https://github.com/CopilotKit/generative-ui)). Google's open-source A2UI is the clearest declarative example. The agent sends a JSON description, and the client renders it with its own native components from "a catalog of trusted, pre-approved components". Google's tagline is "safe like data, but expressive like code". Renderers exist for React, Angular and Lit, with Flutter, SwiftUI and Jetpack Compose named ([google/A2UI](https://github.com/google/A2UI)). Sources disagree on its maturity. Coverage reports v0.8 in production inside Google products, while developer write-ups describe it as still in active development ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/googles-open-standard-lets-ai-agents-build-user-interfaces-on-the-fly/)). Vercel Labs' experimental json-render uses the same idea: a developer-defined catalog of allowed components and actions that the model's JSON output must name ([InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/03/vercel-json-render)). OpenAI's Apps SDK guidelines recommend its own `@openai/apps-sdk-ui` component library for apps rendered inside ChatGPT ([OpenAI UI guidelines](https://developers.openai.com/apps-sdk/concepts/ui-guidelines)). Thesys C1, launched in April 2025, returns streamed UI specs rendered by a React SDK. Its "10x faster" claims are unverified vendor marketing ([Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250418761213/en)).

MCP Apps takes the open-ended approach. Proposed in November 2025 by maintainers from Anthropic, OpenAI and MCP-UI, it merges MCP-UI and the OpenAI Apps SDK into one extension. Pre-declared HTML templates are rendered in sandboxed iframes, hosts can review the HTML and require approval for UI-initiated tool calls, and servers should provide text fallbacks ([MCP blog](https://blog.modelcontextprotocol.io/posts/2025-11-21-mcp-apps/)). Aggregators report it was ratified in January 2026. That is secondhand ([mcp.directory](https://mcp.directory/blog/mcp-apps-standard-vs-openai-apps-sdk-2026)). The announcement does not cover theming or tokens, so how an embedded third-party UI inherits a host's visual language is an open question. Design-system teams are well placed to answer it, for example with host-supplied CSS variables.

The implication is a new deliverable: **a model-facing component catalog**, with strict JSON schemas for props, when-to-use descriptions and safe defaults, maintained next to the human-facing docs. A useful way to see the direction is as three layers. A protocol layer (MCP Apps, A2UI, AG-UI) carries UI intent. A catalog layer, the design system expressed as schemas, defines what can be said. A knowledge layer (skills, docs, MCP servers) tells agents how to say it well. Design-system teams own the second layer and increasingly the third. Big Medium's *Sentient Design* (Josh Clark and Veronika Kindred, Rosenfeld Media, June 2026) supplies the experience-design rationale for runtime-adaptive interfaces, and pairs "practical magic" with a section on defensive design ([Rosenfeld Media](https://rosenfeldmedia.com/create-groundbreaking-user-experiences-with-josh-clark-and-veronika-kindreds-new-book-sentient-design-crafting-intelligent-interfaces-with-ai/); [Kirkus](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/josh-clark/sentient-design/print/)).

### Agents are users too, and they read the accessibility tree

The other direction of agent-first design is building interfaces that agents operate. In April 2026, NN/g's Sarah Gibbons and Kate Moran argued that "user is no longer synonymous with human". Agents act on interfaces to pursue goals, and they perceive them in three ways: screenshots (costly and error-prone), **accessibility-tree parsing (cheaper and more reliable)** and direct APIs. NN/g's recommendations will be familiar to any accessibility practitioner: descriptive labels, no icon-only buttons or vague link text, predictable patterns, markup that mirrors visual grouping, and semantic HTML and ARIA. In their words, "Investing in accessibility has been the right thing to do, but now there's a clear business case for it" ([NN/g](https://nngroup.com/articles/ai-agents-as-users)). Microsoft's Playwright MCP and browser-use both work from accessibility snapshots. Unlabeled inputs appear with empty names, and div-based custom components appear as "generic" rather than "button", which breaks agents ([Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/the-accessibility-tree-is-how-ai-agents-read-your-site-its-breaking/578171/); [InfoWorld](https://infoworld.com/article/4193332/accessibility-is-the-first-class-interface-for-ai-agents.html)). Design systems own semantics at the component level, so each component's accessibility contract (role, name, state, keyboard model) now doubles as its agent API. A component that renders as a div is an agent-breaking bug, not just accessibility debt. No named design system yet documents "agent readability" as a component requirement, and running a browser agent through key flows and inspecting the accessibility snapshot is an obvious QA step no one has written up.

### Design systems are standardising AI's own patterns

Alongside making systems readable by AI, teams are adding components *for* AI features. IBM's Carbon for AI is the most mature documented example. It is a distinct, light-based visual language (brightness, glow, gradients) designed to coexist with Carbon themes. Its first coded component was the **AI label**, which reveals explainability information on click and which Carbon calls "the path to explainability" ([Carbon for AI](https://carbondesignsystem.com/guidelines/carbon-for-ai/); [AI label](https://carbondesignsystem.com/components/ai-label/usage/)). The approach has critics. Supernova notes that Carbon's AI variants show a blue gradient and border even by default, which makes the active component hard to identify. That raises a real question of whether AI should look visually distinct or blend in ([Supernova](https://www.supernova.io/blog/top-6-examples-of-ai-guidelines-in-design-systems)). Emily Campbell's Shape of AI pattern library offers a shared vocabulary, with categories such as identifiers, wayfinders, prompts and tuners ([Prototypr](https://prototypr.io/toolbox/shape-of-ai)). Labelling AI-generated content is the first AI pattern most systems standardise. Approvals, plan previews, progress states and undo are the next layer, and the protocols increasingly assume them, as with MCP Apps' approval of UI-initiated tool calls ([MCP blog](https://blog.modelcontextprotocol.io/posts/2025-11-21-mcp-apps/)). The research could not confirm the current state of Microsoft Fluent's Copilot patterns, Salesforce's agentic patterns or Google PAIR's guidebook, so they are not characterised here.

### Context engineering is the discipline behind all of this

Anthropic's September 2025 post defined context engineering as "the set of strategies for curating and maintaining the optimal set of tokens (information) during LLM inference". It covers system prompts, tools, examples, retrieved data and memory, not just the prompt ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents)). Its principles map almost one-to-one onto the design-system practices above. A finite attention budget means aiming for the "smallest possible set of high-signal tokens", which is why Storybook shows three stories rather than all of them. Instructions should sit at the "right altitude", neither brittle logic nor vague guidance, which is why Figma prefers imperative rules to principles. A few "diverse, canonical examples" beat exhaustive edge cases. Just-in-time retrieval through lightweight identifiers is exactly what MCP servers and skills provide. A design system that pastes its whole documentation site into context breaks the discipline's core principle, which is why Indeed's Markdown setup and Atlassian's DESIGN.md test performed as they did. Design-system teams have become context providers, and context engineering is the closest thing their new work has to a theory.

### Agent skills package judgement where MCP serves data

Anthropic launched Agent Skills in October 2025 and published them as an open standard at agentskills.io in December 2025 ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/ai/anthropic-launches-enterprise-agent-skills-and-opens-the-standard); [Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2025/dec/19/agent-skills)). A skill is a folder of instructions, scripts and resources that loads on demand. Secondary sources report adoption by GitHub Copilot, VS Code, Cursor, OpenAI Codex and Gemini CLI, among others ([Unite.AI](https://www.unite.ai/anthropic-opens-agent-skills-standard-continuing-its-pattern-of-building-industry-infrastructure)). A single uncorroborated source says skills were donated to the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation in October 2026 ([pasqualepillitteri.it](https://pasqualepillitteri.it/en/news/26441/anthropic-agent-skills-linux-foundation-en)). Skills have quickly become a design-system delivery channel. Primer ships 14. Atlassian, Carbon, Ant Design, Spectrum and shadcn ship them. v0 stores imported design systems as skills ([designsystems.one](https://www.designsystems.one/ai-ready/agent-files); [v0](https://v0.app/docs/design-systems-2)). The division of labour that is emerging is that MCP servers and CLIs serve live, queryable data, such as a component's current props, while skills package *judgement*: which pattern to use when, brand voice, review checklists. Atlassian's test showed both reaching about 80% design-system context use, and a mature team will likely ship both from the same source.

## A sequence the evidence supports for an enterprise team

The sources agree, implicitly, on an order of operations, and the order matters more than the tools. zeroheight's "documentation first, MCP second" is the right headline. The evidence suggests a more specific sequence.

Start by auditing the system as an agent would see it. Check that token names are semantic and carry `$description` intent. Check that component and prop descriptions state their purpose and name the alternative. Check that what exists and what doesn't is explicit, and that docs match the current API. Miro's preparation was exactly this: auditing docs, writing icon descriptions and adding best-practice metadata fields ([Substack](https://intodesignsystems.substack.com/p/miro-ai-design-system)). Next, add the deterministic guardrails, token and deprecation lint with fix-naming messages and accessibility tests. They cost no context, they help human developers too, and they catch what every other layer misses. Then generate the machine-readable layer from code: a component manifest or custom-elements manifest and DTCG token JSON, published with each release. Then write a short, always-on router file that states foundation rules and *mandates* calling the on-demand channel, and check that every agent tool in your organisation actually loads it. Only then build the server or CLI. Keep it to a handful of read-only tools over the generated data, with small responses and explicit not-found errors, version-pinned, and with writes kept in a separate server. Measure it with a small internal benchmark of real tasks, read the transcripts, and track the measures vendors don't publish: code-review acceptance and the share of generated UI built from system components. Throughout, treat the accessibility contract of each component as its agent API, and treat component structure as a context problem: if a component is too complex for an agent to reason about, layering it may help more than documenting it.

For enterprises in particular, two choices deserve early attention. First, multi-framework estates hit tooling limits quickly. Storybook's manifest support varies by framework, and Figma's Professional plan allows one Code Connect framework, so the code package, not the Figma library, should be the source of truth. Second, shadow AI is already producing off-system UI, so the first audience for agent-ready context is often not the design-system team's own work but every other team's agents. Shipping the context through the channels those teams already use (Copilot instructions, Cursor rules, Figma Make kits, a portable DESIGN.md) may matter more than building the most elegant server.

## Conclusion

The deepest change here is not a new artifact but a new definition of documentation quality. For a decade, design-system documentation was judged by whether a person could find and understand guidance. It is now also judged by whether a machine can retrieve the right slice cheaply, act on it without asking a follow-up question, and be checked automatically afterwards. That favours precision over persuasion, generation over hand-maintenance, and enforceable rules over principles. It also moves the design-system team toward platform engineering and policy-writing. The most mature example, Atlassian, is notable less for its MCP server than for having one typed source feeding six channels and a benchmark to compare them. Its finding that agents preferred a CLI is a warning against treating "build an MCP server" as the strategy, when it is one output of a strategy.

The open question is whether the grounding works as well as its advocates claim. The field's positive evidence is self-reported and concentrated in a few venues, while its independent evidence documents that generative UI misstates its own rationale, fails accessibility basics and converges aesthetically. The agent-first bet is that a rich design system is the corrective for all three. That bet is reasonable and largely untested. The teams best placed to settle it are enterprise teams willing to publish their own before-and-after benchmarks. A design-system team that builds the measurement alongside the machine-readable layer will be able to show whether its system makes agent output better.
